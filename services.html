What Is a Therapy Approach?

A therapy approach simply means the type or method of therapy a clinician uses to help you. Different approaches focus on different parts of your experience—such as thoughts, emotions, relationships, past trauma, behavior, or the body’s response to stress.

+

For example, a military veteran affected by traumatic experiences may benefit from trauma-focused therapy, while someone struggling with constant worry may find CBT helpful. A couple experiencing repeated conflict may need a relationship-based approach, while someone who feels overwhelmed by intense emotions may benefit from DBT.

These are only examples. People with similar difficulties may need different support depending on their experiences, personality, goals, and current situation. Your therapist may use one approach, combine several, or adjust your treatment as your needs change.

You do not need to know which type of therapy to choose. Tell us what you are going through, and we will help you find a therapist and approach that feel right for you.

Approaches Our Therapists Use

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

Understand how your thoughts, emotions, and behaviors influence one another.

+

CBT helps you recognize unhelpful patterns, develop more balanced ways of thinking, and practice responses that better support your goals.

Often helpful for: Anxiety • Panic • Depression • Stress • Perfectionism • Low self-worth

Learn About CBT →

Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)

Build practical skills for navigating intense emotions.

+

DBT combines acceptance with change. It offers concrete tools for managing distress, regulating emotions, communicating effectively, and responding more intentionally.

Often helpful for: Emotional overwhelm • Impulsivity • Relationship conflict • Distress tolerance • Mood instability

Learn About DBT →

Acceptance & Commitment Therapy (ACT)

Make room for difficult emotions while moving toward what matters.

+

ACT helps you respond differently to uncomfortable thoughts and feelings so they no longer have to control your choices. The focus is on flexibility, values, and meaningful action.

Often helpful for: Anxiety • Depression • Burnout • Avoidance • Grief • Life transitions

Learn About ACT →

Internal Family Systems (IFS)

Understand the different parts of yourself with curiosity instead of judgment.

+

IFS-informed therapy explores how protective patterns developed and what they may be trying to accomplish. This can create greater self-compassion, clarity, and internal balance.

Often helpful for: Trauma • Shame • Self-criticism • Attachment wounds • Identity • Emotional avoidance

Learn About IFS →

Trauma-Focused Therapy

Process trauma safely and change the beliefs that keep it affecting your life.

+

Our clinicians may use Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT), Trauma-Focused CBT, EMDR, or an integrated trauma-informed approach based on your age, experiences, symptoms, and readiness.

Often helpful for: PTSD • Complex trauma • Childhood trauma • Trauma-related guilt or shame • Avoidance • Feeling unsafe

Explore Trauma Treatment →

Gottman Method & Emotionally Focused Therapy

Understand the pattern beneath relationship conflict and build a stronger connection.

+

These evidence-based couples approaches help partners improve communication, manage conflict, repair emotional injuries, and create greater trust and security.

Often helpful for: Recurring arguments • Emotional distance • Trust concerns • Intimacy • Attachment injuries • Relationship repair

Explore Couples Therapy →

Attachment-Based Therapy

Explore how earlier relationships may influence the way you connect today.

+

Attachment-based therapy helps you understand patterns involving trust, closeness, boundaries, vulnerability, and emotional safety—without assuming your past has to determine your future.

Often helpful for: Relationship patterns • Fear of abandonment • People-pleasing • Difficulty trusting • Childhood wounds • Self-worth

Learn About Attachment-Based Therapy →

Somatic Therapy & Nervous-System Regulation

Work with the body’s response to stress—not only your thoughts.

+

Somatic and nervous-system-informed approaches help you notice physical responses, regulate emotional activation, and build a greater sense of safety and resilience.

Often helpful for: Trauma responses • Anxiety • Panic • Chronic stress • Burnout • Emotional shutdown or overwhelm

Care Based on Research—Adapted to You

What Does Evidence-Based Therapy Mean?

Evidence-based therapy means using methods that have been studied and shown to help people with particular mental health concerns. It gives your therapist a reliable starting point—but it does not mean following the same fixed plan with every client.

Your therapist will consider what you are experiencing, what may be contributing to it, how it affects your daily life, and what you want to change. They will then choose or combine approaches that are appropriate for your needs.

Throughout therapy, you and your therapist can discuss what feels helpful, review your progress, and adjust the treatment when needed. Your background, relationships, culture, identity, strengths, boundaries, and preferred pace are all part of that process.

Evidence guides the treatment. Your individual experience shapes how it is used.
